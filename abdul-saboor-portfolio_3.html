Stage 01

Availability & inventory

Before anything can be promised, the business has to know what it actually has, where it sits, and who it belongs to.

INV-01 · built from zero

Inventory management system for a marketplace

GCC omni-channel retail marketplace · Senior PM, fulfillment technology

Problem
A marketplace lets several sellers list the same product from different locations. Treating a SKU as the unit of inventory breaks immediately — you cannot tell whose stock you just sold, or which warehouse owes the customer.
Approach
  • Defined an offer-level identity model — product, supplier and location combined into one key — so the same SKU could be listed by multiple sellers without collision.
  • Modelled stock movement as a transaction ledger: receipts, issues, transfers and adjustments, each with a reason code, rather than mutating a quantity field.
  • Set the system up as the single source of truth other systems subscribe to, instead of one more integration in a mesh.
Outcome
Shipped as the inventory backbone for the marketplace, with real-time positions replacing periodic file syncs.
  • Data modelling
  • Systems design
  • Marketplace
  • Zero to one

INV-02

Availability, allocation and reservation

GCC omni-channel retail marketplace · Senior PM, fulfillment technology

Problem
Overselling and stockouts are the same failure seen from two sides: the number the storefront shows is not the number the warehouse can actually pick.
Approach
  • Specified a global available-to-promise calculation covering every location and every offer, exposed in real time.
  • Added rule-based ring-fencing so stock could be held back for flash sales, priority orders or campaigns without cloning inventory records.
  • Designed a two-step reservation handshake — soft at order creation, hard on warehouse acceptance — with time and event-based release so abandoned carts return stock automatically.
Outcome
Storefront availability and warehouse reality converged; campaign stock could be committed without taking it off sale.
  • Real-time systems
  • ATP
  • Edge cases
  • Commercial trade-offs

INV-03

One inventory picture across five source types

GCC omni-channel retail marketplace · Senior PM, fulfillment technology

Problem
Stock lived in a third-party logistics provider, owned warehouses, retail back rooms and seller systems — each with its own schema, update cadence and definition of "available".
Approach
  • Built an integration layer that harmonised roughly forty inventory attributes into one model, marking each field mandatory or optional by source.
  • Chose publish-subscribe over point-to-point so order management, cart and search consumed one feed rather than four.
  • Sequenced the rollout by source type, deliberately taking the third-party provider first because it was the only one already API-addressable.
Outcome
Unlocked fulfilled-from-store and fulfilled-by-platform models that were previously blocked on inventory visibility.
  • Integration architecture
  • Phased delivery
  • Dependency management
Stage 02

Production & warehouse

The floor is where cost per order is actually won or lost — in labour minutes, walking distance, and how often something has to be done twice.

PRD-01 · flagship

Build-to-order fulfillment

European meal-kit business, three continents · Product lead, supply chain technologies

Problem
Under a build-to-stock model, every additional menu option needs its own pre-built inventory and its own share of finite line capacity. Menu size — the company's main growth lever — was capped by the physical shape of the warehouse.
Approach
  • Reframed the constraint: the ceiling was in the fulfillment model, not the buildings, so the fix belonged in production software rather than capital expenditure.
  • Designed the production capability to assemble to the order instead of drawing from pre-built stock, and worked through what that changed for picking, sequencing and quality control.
  • Delivered against live sites, where the cost of getting it wrong is measured in missed customer boxes.
Outcome
Menu expansion decoupled from line capacity. Adopted as the company's top strategic objective, contributing directly to top-line growth.
  • Strategy
  • Constraint reframing
  • Operating model change
  • Executive alignment

PRD-02

Production control system and floor tooling

European meal-kit business, three continents · Product lead, supply chain technologies

Problem
Distribution centres on three continents run the same product on different building shapes, labour markets and volumes. One global platform ignores that; per-site software is unmaintainable.
Approach
  • Own the roadmap for the production control system and the tools around it — picking applications, quality control, electronic shelf labels for replenishment and pick accuracy, and production dashboards for site leadership.
  • Hold the roadmap against four operating metrics: labour minutes per box, error rate, boxes per hour, and how far the menu can scale.
  • Draw the line between what must be global and what sites configure themselves, deliberately, rather than letting it be settled by whoever escalates loudest.
Outcome
A single production platform in use across Europe, North America and ANZ, with every launch instrumented against at least one floor metric.
  • Platform strategy
  • Labour efficiency
  • Global rollout
  • Operational KPIs

WMS-01 · built from zero

Omni-channel warehouse management system

GCC omni-channel retail marketplace · Senior PM, fulfillment technology

Problem
Off-the-shelf warehouse systems assume an order is fulfilled from one warehouse. The business had staked itself on orders assembled from stores, warehouses and partners at once — and the platforms that could be adapted cost more than building.
Approach
  • Made and documented the build-versus-buy case, including what buying would have bought us and why the hypothesis was too unproven to justify the price.
  • Defined the full process scope — receiving, quality check, putaway, replenishment, picking, consolidation, packing, sortation, dispatch and returns — across fulfillment centres, delivery stations, retail stores and bonded cross-border sites.
  • Planned execution backwards from the critical pull time, so the system scheduled every upstream step against the cut-off and flagged delays before they reached the customer.
  • Cut hardware cost by specifying consumer devices with clip-on infrared scanners in place of industrial handhelds.
  • Shipped as a mobile app for the floor and a web console for warehouse managers, API-first so third-party hardware and software could attach.
Outcome
Built to a design point of 1.5K orders a day, half a million items in storage, 99.5% delivered in full and on time, and fulfillment inside 24 hours.
  • Build vs buy
  • WMS
  • Process design
  • Hardware strategy
  • Chain of custody

Stage 03

Promise & planning

The delivery date shown at checkout is the most consequential number in e-commerce. It sets the customer's expectation and commits the network to meeting it.

PRM-01

Dynamic delivery promise

Omnichannel retail group, MENA · Senior PM, e-commerce supply chain

Problem
A fixed promise is wrong twice over: too cautious for customers near a stocked warehouse, too optimistic for everyone else. Both cost money — one in lost conversion, the other in failed deliveries.
Approach
  • Built the promise from real signals — where the stock is, which site can fulfil, the cut-off still reachable, and the customer's actual location — rather than a static table.
  • Added location intelligence to improve address accuracy in a region where addressing is genuinely hard.
Outcome
Same-day and next-day availability up 14%; failed deliveries down 4.7%; NPS up 0.7.
  • Delivery promise
  • Location intelligence
  • Conversion
  • Cost to serve

PRM-02

Network planning and distribution optimization

GCC omni-channel retail marketplace · Senior PM, fulfillment technology

Problem
A mixed fleet of owned drivers and third-party carriers, planned by hand, cannot be reasoned about — you can see the cost but not which decision caused it.
Approach
  • Owned the planning platform end to end: routing, delivery zones, capacity planning and slot optimization.
  • Worked directly with engineering on the optimization approach and, more importantly, on the operational constraints it had to respect to be usable.
Outcome
Service levels, promise accuracy and cost per order became measurable and steerable rather than emergent.
  • Network optimization
  • Routing
  • Capacity planning
  • 3PL
Stage 04

Last mile & peak

The only part of fulfillment the customer sees — and the part that breaks first when volume spikes.

LM-01

Last-mile platform suite across 11 countries

Regional grocery and hypermarket operator, MENA · Product manager, logistics

Problem
Eleven countries, each with its own carriers, licensed tooling and definition of a delivery — and a growing subscription bill for software that did not talk to itself.
Approach
  • Led development of carrier management, delivery management, delivery promise and customer order tracking as one connected suite.
  • Built predictive analytics and routing optimization on top, so planning improved as the data accumulated.
Outcome
Deployed across eleven countries, replacing licensed tooling for AED 4.8M in annual savings while improving the tracking experience.
  • Multi-country
  • Carrier management
  • Cost reduction
  • Predictive analytics

LM-02

Peak trading at four times volume

Omnichannel retail group, MENA · Senior PM, e-commerce supply chain

Problem
The region's largest trading event compresses a month of orders into days. Everything that is merely adequate in normal weeks fails, and it fails in public.
Approach
  • Owned platform readiness and launch execution end to end, working backwards from the volume the network had to survive.
  • Treated the constraint as the design input rather than testing against normal load and hoping.
Outcome
Sustained four times normal transaction volume without service degradation.
  • Peak readiness
  • Launch execution
  • Scale testing
Stage 05

Returns

Reverse logistics is where customer trust is either repaired or lost for good, and it is almost always the least designed part of the chain.

RET-01

Returns and order journey redesign

Omnichannel retail group, MENA · Senior PM, e-commerce supply chain

Problem
A three-day refund turnaround generates a contact for every return. The customer is not chasing money so much as chasing certainty, and every one of those contacts costs twice — once in service, once in trust.
Approach
  • Redesigned returns, address management and order journeys across web and mobile as one flow rather than three screens.
  • Attacked the refund timeline itself instead of adding status messaging on top of a slow process.
Outcome
Refund turnaround cut from 72 hours to under 24; customer contacts down 11%; satisfaction up.
  • Reverse logistics
  • Customer experience
  • Web & mobile
  • Contact reduction

B2B marketplace with embedded finance

Stealth startup, GCC · Fractional chief product officer
